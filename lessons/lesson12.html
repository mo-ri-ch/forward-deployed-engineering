Interview practice

What FDE interviews look like

FDE interviews test the same two skill sets as the job: engineering, and looking after the customer.

Most have a coding round, a case where you break down a vague business problem, an AI system design round, a role-play with an unhappy client, and questions about your past projects.

Sort each interview question by its round

The case round worries people most.

The decomposition case

The interviewer gives you a vague problem, like "A hospital wants AI. Where do you start?", and watches how you think.

You already know the steps: they're Lesson 2. Ask questions before answering, and say your thinking out loud.

Practise a case

Here's a real-style case. Pick the answer a strong candidate would give at each step.

There's no single right design. Interviewers grade how you get there: questions first, numbers, a small first version, and honest risks.

"A hospital wants AI to help with appointment calls."

Cases often need quick estimates.

✏️ Practice 12.1

Remember:
To estimate, break the number into parts you can guess, then multiply. Say each guess out loud.
1. The hospital gets 3,000 calls a day, and 30% go unanswered. How many is that a day?
2. Each call takes staff 3 minutes. If the system handles 600 calls a day, how many staff hours does that free up a day?
3. With 900 unanswered calls a day and 22 working days, how many unanswered calls is that a month?

AI system design

In the design round you sketch the whole system. Interviewers check that you remember the unglamorous parts: protecting data, checking replies, and watching it after launch.

You built every one of these pieces for Northstar. Now put them in the order a message passes through.

Now, an unhappy client.

The client role-play

An interviewer plays an upset client. They want to see you stay calm, take ownership, and turn the problem into a plan.

Don't argue, and don't hide behind "AI makes mistakes".

The "client" is upset

Last: your own project.

Tell your project's story

"Tell me about a project" comes up in every interview. Have your capstone story ready, in four parts: the problem, what you did, the result as a number, and what you learned.

Keep it to about two minutes. They'll ask follow-up questions on whatever interests them.
